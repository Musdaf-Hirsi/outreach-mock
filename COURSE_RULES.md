# Course Rules Reference

Extracted rules and benchmarks distilled from the IMA course, for internal reference by the outreach-mock codebase. Paraphrased summaries only — not course source text.

Scope: influencer discovery, qualification, drafting, review, follow-up, negotiation and pricing. Brand-side-only outreach content is out of scope for now, per current project focus.

---

## 1. Influencer Qualification

- Vet on four dimensions before ever drafting an email: engagement quality, posting consistency, audience demographics, and communication responsiveness.
- Engagement rate benchmark: 3–8% is healthy for smaller creators; large accounts (1M+ views) are considered healthy around 3%, since engagement naturally declines with scale.
- Engagement floor: below ~2% engagement is a red flag regardless of niche.
- Comment ratio as a fake-engagement tell: very low comments relative to views/likes (course cites under ~1%) suggests bought followers or bot activity — likes are easy to fake at scale, real comments are harder to fake.
- Posting consistency: at least monthly with a predictable cadence is the bar. Sporadic, bursty posting (long gaps then a flurry) signals a hobbyist, not a reliable campaign partner — brands need predictable delivery windows.
- Fluctuating view counts across recent uploads (e.g., wildly inconsistent numbers rather than a steady range) is treated as a red flag distinct from average engagement — inconsistency itself undermines reliability for a campaign.
- Audience geography: for English-language brand campaigns, look for at least one of the "four golden countries" (USA, UK, Canada, Australia) in the top 3 audience countries. None of these present is a strong red flag. For non-English markets, apply the equivalent logic to the target market's own strong-purchasing-power countries (e.g., Spain for the Spanish market, not just any Spanish-speaking country).
- Watch for false-positive geography: a European-language creator whose actual majority audience comes from a much lower-purchasing-power country that happens to share the language (course's specific example: Portuguese-speaking audience skewing Brazilian rather than Portuguese) is still a red flag despite matching on language.
- Age of audience must fit the product/niche being pitched — a niche skewing too young for the product category being considered is a disqualifier regardless of other metrics.
- A surprisingly "easy" or unusually cheap creator (rarely gets sponsorship interest, accepts anything) is itself a soft red flag — often correlates with weak content or a bad-fit audience, not just modesty.
- Responsiveness during initial contact is treated as predictive: if it takes days to hear back at the discovery stage, expect the same lag once a brand deal is on the line — worth testing before investing more time.
- Rating/tracking discipline: keep a running qualitative rating (course suggests a simple star scale) per creator in a tracking sheet so quality signal isn't lost across a growing list.
- Quality over quantity as an operating principle: a small number of excellent, easy-to-sell creators is worth more than a large list of mediocre ones — the course explicitly discourages optimizing for raw creator count.

## 2. Discovery Techniques

- Long-tail, specific search phrasing surfaces smaller/fresher creators; broad generic niche keywords keep resurfacing the same big, already-oversaturated names.
- Once a good small creator is found, reusing their exact video title as a new search query surfaces other creators making similar content — a chaining technique for expanding a list organically.
- YouTube search-operator tricks to bypass algorithmic "what most people mean" matching and get literal keyword matches: quoted exact-phrase search, and an `intitle:` style operator to match words that literally appear in the title.
- Recency bias trick: filtering search results toward recently uploaded content (via UI filters or URL date parameters) surfaces fresher/smaller channels instead of evergreen big-name videos that dominate un-filtered results.
- Sidebar/related-video chaining: once a fitting creator's video is open, the platform's "related to this video" suggestions are a low-effort way to surface more creators in the same specific style, not just the same broad niche.
- Resetting personalization (clearing watch/search history, using a private/incognito session) is recommended when search results keep resurfacing the same known creators, since results are otherwise skewed by prior browsing history.
- Cross-platform expansion: for niches that are strong on Instagram/TikTok as well as YouTube, discovery shouldn't stop at YouTube — the same long-tail-keyword logic is meant to carry over.
- Instagram-specific technique: checking a target brand's own "Tagged" content is a way to directly surface creators that brand has already worked with or featured.
- Paid discovery tools are mentioned as optional accelerators (typically used via free trial): one is a general influencer-marketing-platform-style tool requiring a business email signup; another is described as a browser extension that integrates directly into YouTube for in-context filtering. The course's own conclusion is that the free/manual method above is sufficient and these are not required.
- Baseline subscriber/view range as a starting filter: roughly 50,000–100,000 followers and a minimum ~50,000 average views per video is the course's stated starting criterion, with the explicit caveat that larger creators are also fine — there's no course-stated ceiling.

## 3. Email Drafting

- Fixed four-part structure for every outreach email, in this exact order: Subject Line → Personalization → Offer (with a short bridging sentence connecting personalization to the offer) → Call-to-Action.
- Subject line must avoid generic, overused templates (the equivalent of "Partnership Opportunity" or "Quick question") since recipients pattern-match and skip these; effective subject lines are either short curiosity-triggering one-liners, or a specific detail pulled directly from the email's own personalization.
- Personalization must reference one specific, non-generic detail proven to come from actually watching/reading the creator's content — a sentence that could be copy-pasted to any other creator in the niche unchanged is treated as a failure, not personalization.
- Recommended personalization-sourcing techniques: watch several minutes of a recent video looking for one genuinely commentable moment; check top (most-liked) comments for a resonant theme; check the video's intro/outro; check view-count "spike" moments in the engagement timeline for a viral or notable moment to reference.
- Explicit caution against AI-generated personalization in early stages: generic AI-produced video summaries are described as recognizable and off-putting to experienced recipients; the course recommends learning to write these manually before leaning on AI tools.
- The offer section should lead with a real, findable brand name whenever possible — built by researching what brands nearby/comparable creators in the niche have already worked with (via recent video descriptions/sponsor mentions), then naming 1–2 brands the target creator specifically has *not* yet worked with. This is explicitly preferred over vague "great opportunity" language.
- Never fabricate having existing brand relationships or a client roster that doesn't exist — frame outreach as actively building/onboarding for real brand opportunities in the space, not as an already-closed deal, if no real brand names are available yet.
- One idea and one question per email — never stack multiple asks or multiple disconnected points before the call-to-action.
- Call-to-action must be a single, clear yes/no-style question (e.g., "Are you interested?" / "What do you think?"). Avoid a CTA like "should I send more details?" unless the details actually accompany that same email.
- Target length: roughly 50 words as an ideal, with 100 words treated as a hard outer limit; a "squint test" (stepping back and looking at the email's visual block shape) is recommended to catch anything reading as visually bulky/long before sending.
- Formatting constraints for deliverability and tone: avoid heavy whitespace/line breaks (can trigger spam filters, especially on a young/unestablished sending domain), no links, no decorative signatures or logos — sign-off should be "Best regards" / "Kind regards" plus a first name only.
- Mindset framing before drafting: the sender should write as a professional peer offering value, not as a fan — avoid overt compliments/flattery, avoid language implying the sender needs the deal more than the creator does, and never place the creator on a pedestal (e.g., "I'd be honored," "your content is amazing").
- The "already actively working on this" framing (positioning as an existing outreach effort or onboarding process rather than a tentative cold ask) is treated as more credible and reduces "who are you / what's your agency" friction — but this must never cross into fabricating specific false claims (see brand-naming rule above).

## 4. Supervisor / Quality Auditing

- Reject any subject line that's a generic, interchangeable template line rather than something curiosity-driven or personalization-derived.
- Reject any email that reads as copy-pasteable to any other creator in the niche unchanged — genuine, checkable specificity is a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have.
- Reject fabricated credibility claims: naming specific closed partnerships, specific brand relationships, or a client roster not actually backed by real information available to the sender.
- Reject needy or over-eager tone: excessive enthusiasm reacting to a stated price/offer (e.g., visible excitement at a number given), over-complimenting, or language that reads as the sender being more invested in getting the deal than the recipient is.
- Reject emails that stack more than one question/idea, or where the call-to-action isn't cleanly a yes/no ask.
- Reject formatting violations: links, decorative signature blocks/logos, or anything beyond a plain "Best/Kind regards + first name" sign-off.
- Reject length violations: hard cap around 100 words, with ~50 as the target — treat anything visibly bulky as a failure independent of exact word count.
- First impressions are treated as effectively unrecoverable in tone: a message that reads as unprofessional, sloppy, or overly generic("who is this guy") is expected to make every subsequent negotiation harder, since it changes how much the recipient trusts the sender's competence and seriousness.
- "Good vs bad" patterns to specifically watch for in review, drawn from real flagged examples: agreeing to a call/next step before qualifying the recipient's actual value (their audience, analytics, recent post performance); visibly celebrating or expressing excitement when someone states their asking price (a major tell of inexperience/neediness that invites being taken advantage of in negotiation); writing in a flat, boring, or overly transactional tone with no personality (reads as "will be a mess to work with" even before any actual issue occurs); vague, unquantified claims like "we have a few brands in mind" without any specific detail, especially stacked across multiple ambiguous points in one message.

## 5. Follow-up Logic

- Expected reply rate on a cold first email alone is roughly 3–5%; combining consistent follow-ups with well-written emails is described as capable of pushing this closer to 7–15%. This is reply rate to the *first* message only, not an eventual close rate.
- Never follow up the same day as the original send — wait at minimum a full working day.
- If the original email was sent on a non-working day (or late Friday), don't count that day (or the following Monday) as the elapsed working day — wait until the next genuine working day has passed before the first follow-up, since Mondays are considered a poor response day generally (inbox backlog from the weekend).
- Escalating cadence across follow-ups: roughly 1 working day before the first follow-up (first message only), then roughly 2+ working days between each subsequent follow-up, loosening further after multiple attempts.
- Tuesday and Thursday are called out as empirically better response days than other weekdays (course's own experience, not a hard rule); Friday sends/follow-ups are discouraged since recipients are mentally checked out for the weekend.
- Never follow up on weekends.
- Bad-practice pattern explicitly called out: following up daily or many times within a single week — reads as spam/desperation and risks damage to sender reputation (spam-flagging).
- A follow-up's only content goal is to get the recipient to read/respond to the *original* message — it should never introduce a new ask, a new question, or new information; it's purely a nudge/bump.
- Suggested follow-up escalation script structure (explicitly stated as inspiration, not to be copy-pasted verbatim since templates that circulate widely get flagged): a light "just checking you saw this" nudge → a slightly more direct confirmation-style nudge → an explicit "bumping to top of inbox" framing → adding light humor/a meme as a pattern interrupt → openly acknowledging the number of follow-ups sent so far with self-aware humor → a final lighthearted "last check-in" tone.
- Memes/GIFs as follow-up tools: recommended as an occasional pattern interrupt (not on every follow-up) to stay memorable without derailing the professional tone; should complement rather than replace the actual message.
- After roughly 4–6 follow-ups with zero response in one thread, cadence should slow dramatically (e.g., down to about once every one to two weeks) rather than stopping outright or continuing at the same pace.
- If a thread goes fully cold after repeated follow-ups (course cites needing up to 7 attempts as a threshold in one place), the recommended move is a fresh thread with a different subject line and a different angle — e.g., leading with a specific concrete deal instead of a general partnership ask, or mentioning that other creators/opportunities are already in motion — rather than continuing to nudge the dead thread.
- If a specific contact/brand is ghosting entirely, try reaching a different contact at the same organization, or reach out via a different platform (social DM) to ask to be redirected to the right person — framed as being specific and mentioning known relevant details (other creators worked with, competitor brands) to increase credibility.
- If an individual influencer is ghosting, leveraging recent unrelated success (other deals just closed, other brands you're now working with) as a reason to re-open the thread is suggested, rather than only re-asking the same question.
- For brand-side follow-ups specifically (versus influencer-side), tone should be noticeably gentler/less aggressive since the sender is the one asking for something of value from the brand, not the other way around; after multiple unanswered follow-ups with a brand, it's acceptable to explicitly ask whether the current contact is even the right person to be talking to.
- Mid-conversation follow-ups (after some reply has already been received) follow the same core timing logic as first-contact follow-ups, but can be slightly faster/looser if the outstanding ask is simple (e.g., a yes/no question) versus something that requires the recipient to do real work (e.g., preparing pricing or a media kit) — those deserve more patience.

## 6. Negotiation & Pricing

*(Now reflected in the codebase — see `src/pricing/cpm-calculator.ts`, `src/mastra/agents/negotiation-agent.ts`, and the negotiation-state fields in `src/tracking/outreach-log.ts`. Deal type/CPM/cap/monitoring days are prompted for on close in both the CLI (`run-negotiate.ts`) and web negotiate flows, and `npm run set-relationship` marks a creator exclusive — see the cross-reference section below.)*

### Pricing fundamentals

- CPM (cost per thousand views) is the primary pricing anchor across platforms — computed as price ÷ (views ÷ 1000).
- Typical niche CPM ranges cited (YouTube baseline, non-exhaustive, meant as ballpark only): Tech/Gadgets ~$20–50, Finance ~$40–80, Health/Fitness ~$15–35, Entertainment ~$5–20, Lifestyle/Beauty ~$10–25, Travel ~$12–20, Education ~$25–40, Gaming ~$10–20, Food/Cooking ~$10–20, Luxury Goods ~$30–50. Unlisted niches default to ~$5–15. Course explicitly frames these as rough guides, not fixed rules — actual pricing requires developing situational judgment ("a sniff") through practice.
- Two payment structures: flat rate (fixed price regardless of performance — low risk for the creator, all risk to the brand) versus CPM/performance-based (paid per actual views achieved, typically capped at a maximum view count and bounded by a monitoring window, e.g., 30 days) — higher risk/reward for the creator, safer for the brand.
- Positive price-adjustment factors (justify pricing above baseline CPM): recognized niche authority/exclusivity, premium production quality, unusually loyal/high-trust audience.
- Negative price-adjustment factors (justify pricing below baseline CPM): engagement under ~2%, high posting frequency with low effort per post (audience fatigue), inconsistent view counts across recent uploads, inexperienced/new creators who tend to undervalue or overvalue themselves inconsistently.
- Platform CPM scaling relative to YouTube (rough rule of thumb, not precise): Instagram roughly half of YouTube's rate for the same niche; TikTok roughly a third; Facebook similar to or slightly below Instagram; Twitter/X the lowest of all platforms discussed given high impression counts but much lower conversion.
- Standard pricing-calculation procedure: gather the creator's last ~12 pieces of content, drop the single highest and single lowest performer as outliers, average the remaining ~10 for a realistic baseline view count, then multiply that by the niche's baseline CPM to get an estimated fair price.
- Never pitch a brand a lower price than what the creator originally quoted, even after negotiating the creator down internally — always pitch brands at (or above) the original quoted number, keeping the negotiated discount as the agency's margin. Pitching below the original quote sacrifices margin permanently and cannot be corrected upward later without damaging trust.

### Negotiation mechanics

- Negotiation is expected by experienced creators — accepting a first-quoted price outright is treated as a signal of inexperience, not generosity, and undermines the ability to make any margin at all.
- Standard negotiation target: roughly 20–50% off an initial quote for a fairly-priced creator; more aggressive discounts (40–80% off) are reserved for cases assessed as clearly overpriced relative to CPM math.
- Never state your own target price first — always let the other party quote first, then counter from a deliberately low anchor and move toward the real target, rather than starting near the target and having no room left to negotiate.
- Recommended framing style: present budget constraints as an external factor ("the budget for this campaign caps at X") rather than as a judgment on the creator's worth — the course repeatedly warns against language that reads as attacking the creator's value directly, since that provokes defensiveness rather than agreement.
- "Would that be a dealbreaker?" / "Would you be against X?" framing is highlighted as effective — reframing a direct ask as a question the other party would have to actively refuse, which is psychologically harder than passively accepting.
- Recommended cap on negotiation back-and-forth: roughly 2–3 rounds of counter-offers: beyond that, the exchange starts to read as dragging and can damage the relationship regardless of outcome.
- Long-term-partnership framing (implying future repeat deals, not just this one) is called out as a strong lever for getting a creator to accept a lower per-deal rate, since it reframes a single cheap deal as part of a bigger ongoing volume opportunity.
- Never insult, attack, or bluntly reject a stated price — the recommended pattern is to acknowledge the value, then redirect the reason for a lower number to an external constraint, staying warm in tone while remaining firm on the actual number.
- If a creator won't move on price at all, the course recommends not burning the relationship — thank them, note the situation, and keep them as a future prospect, since circumstances (budget, timing, alternative offers) can change later.
- For creators managed by an agent (their email is not personal / has an agency-style domain), the standard approach is to negotiate a lower price with the agent (since the agent already takes their own cut) rather than requesting a commission split, then resell that lower price to the brand at the creator's originally-quoted rate.
- For unmanaged creators, an alternative to a one-off discount is offering exclusive management (an ongoing percentage-based agent relationship) — usually lower margin per individual deal but offers longer-term relationship value, inbound-lead access, and easier brand pitching (some brands specifically prefer/require exclusivity). Course explicitly notes most realized profit historically has come from simple discount-based brokering, not exclusive management, and recommends starting new relationships with a discount arrangement before considering exclusivity.
- Typical commission range for brokered (non-exclusive) discount deals: roughly 20–40%, with the lower end appropriate when starting out or dealing with bigger creators, and the higher end justified once more value-add services are actually being delivered.
- Once a creator responds and is engaged, the recommended flow before naming any price is to ask qualifying questions first (audience demographics/analytics, deal-volume capacity, current rates, recent post performance) — this both genuinely assesses fit and signals professionalism/selectivity rather than desperation, and it surfaces negotiating leverage (e.g., recent underperforming posts, audience-geography mismatches) before any price is discussed.
- A "media kit" (or equivalent screenshot of platform analytics) is the standard artifact requested at this stage — brands will ask for the same data later, so collecting it upfront avoids delay and gives a factual basis for any pricing pushback.

### Cross-niche upselling

- Brands select creators based on the demographic/psychographic profile of the audience (age, geography, interests/lifestyle), not strictly the content's surface-level topic label — a creator whose content sits in one labeled niche can still be a strong fit for a brand in an entirely different category if the underlying audience profile matches what that brand is trying to reach.
- Practical implication: when trying to find additional brands for an already-closed creator (or additional creators for an already-closed brand), it's worth reasoning about the audience's actual demographics/interests rather than only searching within the creator's literal topic label — this is presented as a differentiator most competitors miss by staying rigidly within same-label niche matching.

## 7. Operational Constraints

- YouTube's platform has a practical daily cap on how many times a user can look up a creator's contact email through its available-to-humans lookup flow (captcha-gated) — recommended mitigations: briefly reviewing whether a creator is even worth pursuing before spending a lookup attempt on them; using a separate, dedicated account/browser session for prospecting rather than a personal daily-use account; using incognito/private browsing when completing lookups to reduce IP-based throttling; varying network/IP (e.g., switching between different connections) if limits are still being hit frequently.
- Maintain a running tracking sheet (spreadsheet) as the operational backbone of the whole workflow — used to log candidate creators, their qualification notes/ratings, pricing/media-kit data, outreach dates, and follow-up status, so nothing depends on memory across a growing list.
- Volume is treated as the primary lever for consistent results — the course frames outreach performance as fundamentally a numbers game where technique improves the conversion rate, but insufficient volume is the single most common root cause of a stalled pipeline, more often than a broken technique.
- Recommended operational batching: focus on closing several creators before shifting attention to brand-side outreach (course cites closing at least 2–3 before actively pivoting), rather than interleaving one-off single-creator-then-single-brand cycles — provides negotiating fallback options and avoids single-point-of-failure dependency on one creator's response.
- After a creator is provisionally closed (rates + audience data gathered, willing to work), set and honor a specific timeline for next steps (course suggests roughly 1–3 weeks as a normal outreach-to-brand-match window) and proactively check in even with no news, rather than going silent — recipients tolerate delay much better when an explicit timeframe was set upfront, even if the actual delay is similar either way.
- A creator who follows up unusually often/eagerly after being closed is flagged as a signal of low deal-flow/neediness on their side, which can itself be used as leverage in a later repricing conversation (e.g., proposing a lower rate in exchange for more consistent deal volume) — framed as a mutually-beneficial reframing, not exploitation, since it's presented as a genuine volume-for-price tradeoff.
